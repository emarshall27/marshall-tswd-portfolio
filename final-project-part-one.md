# Outline

### Main Audience: 
National Park Service officials, park managers, and policymakers responsible for managing public lands, visitor access, and conservation efforts.

### Main Message & Call to Action: 
Increasing national park visitation creates growing challenges, including overcrowding, environmental damage, and pressure on park resources. This project explores how tourism affects national parks and the importance of developing management strategies that balance public access with long-term conservation. Encourage park managers and policymakers to prioritize sustainable tourism, invest in park resources, and implement strategies that protect natural environments while keeping national parks accessible for future generations.

### Project Summary: 
This project will explore how increasing tourism and visitation are affecting America's national parks, focusing on overcrowding, environmental damage, and pressure on park resources. Using publicly available visitation data and other supporting information, I want to show how visitor numbers have changed over time, which parks are experiencing the greatest pressure, and what challenges this creates for conservation and park management. This matters because national parks are meant to be protected for future generations, but growing popularity can make that increasingly difficult.
The intended audience is National Park Service officials, park managers, and policymakers responsible for managing public lands. Through data visualizations, maps, and comparisons, I hope to demonstrate the need for better visitor management and sustainable tourism strategies. My ultimate goal is to encourage decision-makers to balance public access with environmental protection.

###One-Sentence Summary: 
Examining how increasing tourism affects America's national parks and why stronger visitor management and conservation strategies are needed to protect them for future generations.


# The data

1) NPS Visitor Use Statistics, The Growing Appeal: Establish how national park visitation has changed over time and which parks attract the most visitors. The National Park Service’s Visitor Use Statistics database will be my primary source for examining how visitation to national parks has changed over time. It includes annual and monthly recreation visit numbers, allowing me to identify trends, compare parks, and determine which locations receive the most visitors. I plan to use this data to create charts showing visitation growth, particularly from 2000 to 2025. One important consideration is that the data counts visits rather than individual visitors, since one person may visit a park multiple times.

National Park Service. “Visitor Use Data.” Social Science. Accessed November 17, 2026. [https://home.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics-dashboard.htm](https://home.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics-dashboard.htm)

Visualization: Line chart showing annual visitation trends from 2000–2025, with comparisons of the most visited parks.


2) NPS Park Boundaries, The Pressure Points: Identify which parks experience the greatest visitor pressure relative to their physical size. I will use the National Park Service’s Administrative Boundaries dataset to show where national parks are located and compare their geographic sizes. This information will help me create a map of the parks included in the project and explore how visitation compares to park size. I may use this information to calculate visitors per square mile as one way to illustrate where visitor pressure could be greatest, while recognizing that this measurement does not directly indicate environmental damage.

National Park Service, Land Resources Division. “Administrative Boundaries of National Park System Units.” October 9, 2026. National Park Service DataStore. [https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2321443](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2321443)

Visualization: Interactive U.S. map showing visitation by park, alongside a comparison of visitors per square mile.


3) NPS Natural Resource Condition Assessments, The Environmental Cost: Explore documented environmental challenges and resource conditions in heavily visited parks. The National Park Service’s Natural Resource Condition Assessments will provide information about the environmental conditions of individual parks. These reports examine resources such as wildlife, vegetation, water quality, and air quality, documenting current conditions and changes over time. I plan to use selected park assessments to provide context for the visitation data and highlight some of the environmental challenges parks face. Since these reports vary by park, I will use them as supporting evidence rather than assume that increased visitation is the direct cause of every environmental issue.

National Park Service. “Natural Resource Condition Assessment Program.” Science. Last updated October 16, 2023. https://www.nps.gov/subjects/science/nrca.htm [https://www.nps.gov/subjects/science/nrca.htm](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/science/nrca.htm)

Visualization: Comparison of selected parks highlighting documented concerns involving wildlife, vegetation, water, and other natural resources.


4) NPS Visitor Surveys, The Visitor Experience: Examine how visitors perceive park conditions and their experiences. The National Park Service’s Socioeconomic Monitoring Visitor Surveys provide information about visitor satisfaction, trip planning, spending, and perceptions of their visits. I plan to use survey results to examine how visitors feel about their experiences, particularly their satisfaction with park conditions and perceptions of crowding. This will help demonstrate that increasing visitation can affect not only the parks themselves but also the experiences of the people visiting them.

National Park Service. “Socioeconomic Monitoring Visitor Surveys.” Social Science. Accessed November 17, 2026. [https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/socioeconomic-monitoring-visitor-surveys.htm](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/socioeconomic-monitoring-visitor-surveys.htm)

Visualization: A stacked bar chart showing the percentage of visitors who reported different levels of satisfaction with their park experience, such as very satisfied, satisfied, and dissatisfied. This will make it easy to compare visitor experiences across selected parks.


5) NPS Visitor Use Management Framework, Finding a Balance: Managing the Growth: Explore how national parks are responding to increasing visitation and what strategies can help protect them. The Interagency Visitor Use Management Framework provides guidance for managing visitor access while protecting natural and cultural resources. I plan to use this framework alongside information from individual park websites to examine management approaches such as reservation systems, visitor limits, shuttle services, and seasonal restrictions. This will help demonstrate the different ways parks are responding to increased visitation and the importance of planning for sustainable tourism.

Interagency Visitor Use Management Council. “Visitor Use Management Framework.” Accessed November 17, 2026. [https://visitorusemanagement.nps.gov/VUM/Framework](https://visitorusemanagement.nps.gov/VUM/Framework) 

Visualization: A donut chart showing the share of selected national parks that use different visitor management approaches, such as reservation systems, shuttle services, or visitor limits. This will provide a simple visual overview of how parks are managing increased visitation.

# Method and medium

I plan to use Tableau & Datawrapper to create the data visualizations and Shorthand to bring everything together into an interactive, standalone story. Tableau will allow me to create charts, maps, and comparisons using the National Park Service data, while Shorthand will help organize the visualizations alongside written explanations, images, and supporting information. The final project will be presented as an interactive webpage that guides readers through the story, from increasing park visitation to the challenges it creates and potential solutions. I want the final product to be visually engaging and easy to navigate, allowing readers to explore the data while understanding the larger message about balancing public access with conservation.
